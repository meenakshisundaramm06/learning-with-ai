# Learning with AI: from material to capability

## Presenter-ready content for a 45-minute remote session

**Audience:** experienced designers learning adjacent skills such as code, product management, research, data, and AI.

**Session promise:** by the end, each person will have a small prerequisite map for one capability they want to build, a credible next learning task, and an AI-coach prompt that helps them practise rather than merely consume.

**Core question:**

> AI and the internet give us more learning material than ever. Why do so many of our learning attempts still end at videos, courses, and saved links?

**Core answer:**

> Access gives us material. Capability comes from the right sequence of effort: build the needed foundations, practise at the edge of what you can do, use feedback to correct a specific gap, and return later without the guide.

In one line:

> AI can remove the cost of asking. It cannot remove the cost of learning.

---

# 1. What the audience should leave with

The session teaches four moves:

```text
MAP THE PATH
Target capability → prerequisites → current frontier
                         ↓
SHRINK THE STEP
One worked example → one manageable new task
                         ↓
PRACTISE AT THE EDGE
Attempt → specific feedback → correction → fresh attempt
                         ↓
RETURN AND MIX
Recall later → vary the context → use it in larger work
```

These four moves combine the useful parts of the source without asking the audience to remember eleven learning-science terms.

One direction rule holds the system together:

> Plan from the outcome backwards. Learn from the first missing prerequisite forwards.

# 3. Detailed facilitator guide

## Segment 1 — The pile of material (0–4 minutes)

### On screen

```text
Courses saved       7
Videos saved       34
Links bookmarked   81

New capability      ?
```

Then reveal:

> AI and the internet give us material. A learning system turns that material into capability.

### Say

> This session started with a problem I have, not a subject I have mastered. I have collected courses, videos, and links for months. I was busy around learning, but I had not changed what I could actually do.
>
> I wanted to learn React. I kept finding better explanations and better courses. But when I tried to build something, I was learning the fundamentals and solving the larger problem at the same time. It was overwhelming.
>
> At times, I read that overwhelm as loss of interest. But a task can become dull when it repeatedly gives us confusion instead of progress. AI has made explanations almost free. The internet has made material abundant. Yet access was not my main problem. I did not have a good sequence for turning an explanation into a capability.

### Ask in chat

> Name one skill you intended to learn and one type of resource you collected for it.

Allow 20–30 seconds. Read out two or three examples without judging them.

### Transition

> If access alone is not enough, what does one-to-one support add?

---

## Segment 2 — The tutoring opportunity (4–9 minutes)

### Visual

Use Bloom's original 1984 chart showing the distributions for conventional instruction, class-based mastery learning, and one-to-one tutoring.

### Say

> In 1984, educational psychologist Benjamin Bloom reported a striking result. In the study he discussed, the average learner in the one-to-one tutoring condition performed above 98% of learners in the conventional class condition.
>
> The chart is not a promise that every tutor—or every AI chat—produces a two-standard-deviation gain. Later reviews find smaller average effects that vary by system and setting. I want to use the chart as a design question: what changes when a good tutor can respond to one learner?

Reveal these one at a time:

```text
A tutor can know:

1. Where you want to go
2. What you can already do
3. Which prerequisite is missing
4. What task should come next
5. What your attempt reveals
6. When to remove help and test again
```

> A tutor does more than answer questions. A tutor manages the learning loop.
>
> AI makes this kind of interaction available on demand. But a general chatbot does not automatically know our real level, choose a sound sequence, make us do the work, or verify its own advice. We have to design that relationship.

### Evidence note for the presenter

Bloom's paper reported the famous two-sigma comparison. A later review by VanLehn found an average effect size of 0.79 for human tutoring and 0.76 for the intelligent tutoring systems it reviewed. A 2016 meta-analysis of 50 intelligent-tutoring evaluations found a median improvement of 0.66 standard deviations, with results sensitive to test alignment and implementation. These studies concern designed tutoring systems, not ordinary general-purpose AI chat. Use the original graph as provocation, not as an AI claim.

## Segment 3 — Support changes with the learner (9–13 minutes)

### Visual

A three-part horizontal progression:

```text
EXPLORE                 BUILD                    REFINE
interest + play         technique + practice     judgment + style

“What is possible?”     “Can I do this well?”    “What is my approach?”
```

Use Bloom's original examples beneath it:

- **Explore:** children play with a sport; coaches encourage effort and interest.
- **Build:** tennis becomes “real business”; coaching focuses on precision, technique, strategy, strengths, and weaknesses.
- **Refine:** accomplished athletes work with master coaches on tactics and personal style; mathematicians tackle unsolved problems.

### Say

> Bloom's talent-development work suggests that support should change as the learner changes.
>
> At the start, exploration and encouragement help us develop interest. In the middle, we need foundations, technique, correction, and repeated practice. Later, the work shifts toward judgment, style, and original contribution.
>
> This matters because we often ask one learning method to do all three jobs. A beginner asks for an open-ended project and gets overwhelmed. An experienced person repeats beginner exercises and gets bored.

### Quick self-check

> For the skill you named, are you exploring it, building reliable foundations, or refining an existing capability?

No group discussion is needed. Ask people to write one word in their own notes.

### Transition

> My React mistake was clearer once I saw this. I had an early-stage foundation and a middle-stage project.

---

## Segment 4 — Why large goals overwhelm us (13–18 minutes)

### Visual 1: the bottleneck

```text
New information          Working memory           Long-term memory

React syntax ───────┐
Components ─────────┤
Props ──────────────┤       [ small workbench ]  ⇄  [ familiar patterns ]
State ──────────────┤
Events ─────────────┤
Accessibility ──────┘
```

### Say

> Working memory is the small workbench where we hold and manipulate unfamiliar information. If the task places too many new pieces on that workbench, the learner cannot assemble them reliably.
>
> Long-term memory changes the situation. Familiar patterns no longer need the same conscious attention. That is why foundations and automaticity matter: they leave room for judgment and creative work.

### Visual 2: the learning staircase

Show one impossible step beside several smaller steps reaching the same height.

### Say

> Reducing a step does not reduce the ambition. Both staircases reach the same height.
>
> “Build an accessible filter panel” is a useful destination. It is a poor first exercise if component structure, JavaScript data operations, React state, and accessible form behaviour are all new.
>
> Those who skip the foundations do not escape them; they meet them later with worse timing.

Keep the destination. Change the next step.

### Transition

> So how do we find the smaller steps without blindly completing every course that mentions React?

---

## Segment 5 — Map the path (18–24 minutes)

### Visual 1: from course to graph

```text
Course model

Lesson 1 → Lesson 2 → Lesson 3 → Lesson 4 → ...


Knowledge-graph model

HTML forms ────────────────┐
CSS layout ────────────────┤
JS functions → arrays ─────┤
                            ├→ filter behaviour → accessible filter panel
components → props → state ┤
labels + keyboard behaviour┘
```

### Say

> A course is a fixed container. A knowledge graph is a map of capabilities and the prerequisites between them.
>
> The arrows answer: “What must I be able to do before this becomes a manageable next step?”
>
> You do not need to master every topic in a broad course before your target. You need the prerequisites that lie on your path.

### Direction and execution

```text
PLAN TOP-DOWN
accessible filter panel ← what does this require? ← what comes first?

LEARN BOTTOM-UP
first missing prerequisite → next skill → accessible filter panel
```

> The outcome helps us decide which branch matters. The actual learning begins at the lowest missing prerequisite and moves forward. A large project can stay visible as the destination and still be the wrong first exercise.

### Visual 2: the frontier

Colour or label the nodes:

```text
[CAN DO] → [CAN DO] → [LEARNING NOW] → [LOCKED FOR NOW]
                         ↑
                 knowledge frontier
```

### Say

> The knowledge frontier is the edge between what you can do reliably and what you are ready to learn next.
>
> A good next task sits on this edge. Its direct prerequisites are in place, but the task still asks you to change your performance.
>
> The map is not a one-time planning exercise. Your attempts test it. If a node stays overwhelming, split it or add the prerequisite you missed. An AI-generated map is only a draft until performance or a trusted source supports it.

### How to make a small knowledge graph

```text
1. Name a target performance.
2. Work backwards: what must I be able to do first?
3. Continue until you reach skills you can already prove.
4. Draw prerequisite → next-skill arrows.
5. Mark: can do / learning now / locked.
6. Trace the target backwards to the first node you cannot yet prove.
7. Choose that frontier node—or, if there are several, the one that unlocks the most useful next steps.
8. Check the map: arrows point from prerequisite to next skill; there are no loops; and broad nodes are split.
```

### Node-quality test

A useful node:

- describes something observable;
- can be practised through a small task;
- has a clear success check;
- is challenging but achievable after a brief example;
- is not the name of a course, resource, or tool.

### Transition

> The graph helps us choose the right thing. We still need to practise it in the right way.

---

## Segment 6 — How capability forms (24–31 minutes)

### Visual: the practice loop

```text
MINIMUM EXPLANATION
one worked example
        ↓
ATTEMPT
make, decide, explain, or solve
        ↓
TARGETED FEEDBACK
find the smallest consequential gap
        ↓
CORRECT
repeat the weak part with less help
        ↓
PROVE
fresh task, no original answer
        ↓
RETURN
later, in a different context
```

### Say

> Active learning does not mean being abandoned with a project. Beginners benefit from direct explanation and worked examples. The support should then fade.
>
> Deliberate practice is not simply spending time on the activity. It is a task chosen to improve one part of performance, followed by feedback and another attempt.
>
> Start with concrete examples. An abstraction becomes useful when it compresses patterns we have already handled; if it arrives first, it may sound clear without changing what we can do.
>
> This is where AI can be useful. It can generate a small example, inspect an attempt, identify a likely missing prerequisite, create a correction exercise, and give a fresh task. It can keep the action–feedback–correction cycle short.
>
> It can also make learning worse. If it supplies the whole answer before we try, it creates fluent output and a passive learner.

### The React example, end to end

```text
Target
Build an accessible filter panel

Frontier skill
Render a list from data in a React component

Worked example
Render three product names from a small array

Learner attempt
Render filter options from a different array

Targeted feedback
Correct the component structure and one data-mapping error

Fresh proof task
Render categories from new data without the original example

Later layer
Add selection state, then integrate it into the filter panel
```

> The larger project stays visible, but the learning task changes one part of performance at a time.

### The reference rule

> Use the guide when you need support. Then close it and reproduce the move. If you are stuck, peek only at the missing piece, close it again, and continue.

### The proof rule

> Do not count “AI explained it” as learning. Count what you can do without the explanation.

### Return practice

Use a light schedule rather than a complex algorithm:

```text
Today      Learn with an example; complete a small attempt
Tomorrow   Recreate or explain without the guide
Later      Use it in a different example
Next week  Use it as part of a larger, real task
```

Explain that the exact interval should adapt to difficulty and performance. The important part is returning after some forgetting and varying the context.

### Transition

> Let us use AI to make the first version of this system for something you actually want to learn.

# 4. Final activity — Build your learning frontier (31–43 minutes)

## Purpose

Each person leaves with:

- a target capability;
- a small prerequisite map;
- one frontier skill;
- one 25-minute practice task;
- a proof check and a repeatable return cue.

This is useful even if they never open the presentation again.

## Activity instructions

### Minutes 1–2: choose the target

Complete:

```text
I want to use __________________ to ______________________________.

Evidence that I can do it will be _______________________________.
```

“Learn React” names a field. “Use React to build an accessible filter panel that works with keyboard and screen-reader input” names a performance.

The target may be ambitious—it is the destination, not today's task. Check that it points to a real use, has visible evidence, and matters enough that you will return to it.

### Minutes 3–6: ask AI for a draft map

Copy this prompt into the AI tool available to you:

> Act as my learning coach. My target capability is **[target]**. Evidence that I can do it will be **[visible evidence]**. I can currently **[what I can already do, with examples]**.
>
> First, ask me no more than three short diagnostic questions. Then propose a small prerequisite graph with 6–8 observable, testable skills. Write every connection as `prerequisite → next skill`. Do not recommend courses or resources. Mark your best guess as `can do`, `learning frontier`, or `locked for now`, and explain the one uncertainty that most affects the map. Ask me to correct the map before continuing.

### Minutes 7–9: correct and validate the map

Ask:

- Is anything marked `can do` that I cannot demonstrate?
- Is a node too broad to practise in one sitting?
- Is there a hidden prerequisite from my past attempts?
- Which node unlocks the most useful next options?
- Does an official curriculum, trusted practitioner, or authoritative source contradict this sequence?

Select one frontier node.

The map is provisional. The first attempt will supply better evidence than the first AI response.

### Minutes 10–12: create the first practice task

Continue with:

> For the frontier skill **[skill]**, give me one minimal worked example and one different 25-minute task. Do not solve the task for me. After I submit my attempt, assess it against 3–5 clear criteria, identify the single most important gap, and give me one correction task. After I correct it, give me a fresh task without the worked example.

Complete the activity card:

```text
Target capability:

My frontier skill:

My first 25-minute attempt:

Evidence of success:

My return cue and task:
After __________________, starting on __________, I will __________.
```

For technical facts, verify the map and feedback with official documentation or a trusted practitioner. For product strategy, organisational context, design taste, ethics, or consequential decisions, include a human reviewer.

### No-AI fallback

If an AI tool is unavailable or slow, draw six boxes yourself. Put the target at the right, work backwards with “What must I be able to do first?”, mark the first uncertain box as the frontier, and write one 25-minute proof task. The method does not depend on the tool.

## Chat share

Ask everyone to post one line:

> My frontier skill is ___, and my first proof task is ___.

Do not ask people to share their full graph. The line is enough to make the plan concrete without using the session for status theatre.

---

# 5. Closing (43–45 minutes)

### On screen

```text
More material is not the answer.

Map the path.
Shrink the step.
Practise at the edge.
Return and mix.
```

### Say

> We began with a pile of courses, videos, and links. Those resources are not useless. They are raw material.
>
> The shift is to stop asking, “What should I consume next?” and ask, “What should I be able to do next, what does it depend on, and what attempt will prove it?”
>
> Plan from the outcome backwards. Learn from the first missing prerequisite forwards.
>
> AI can help map the path, adapt the explanation, create practice, and respond to evidence. It is a multiplier; the base layer is the knowledge and judgment we build. The part that changes us is still the attempt, the correction, and the return.
>
> Before you leave, put your return task on your calendar. Do not rely on remembering that you intended to learn.

Final line:

> AI and the internet give us material. A deliberate learning loop turns that material into capability.

# 6. Presenter notes

## Tone

- Speak as a practitioner testing a better learning system, not as a learning scientist or motivational speaker.
- Use your React experience throughout; it gives the ideas continuity and credibility.
- Let the structure carry the session. You do not need jokes, performance, or constant audience participation.
- State claims plainly. Pause after the two-sigma graph, the staircase, and the knowledge frontier.
- Do not adopt the second source's scolding tone. Treat procrastination and overwhelm as problems to diagnose, then ask for action and evidence.

## If time runs short

Cut in this order:

1. Shorten the three-stage talent-development segment to one minute.
2. Remove the detailed return schedule; keep the rule to return without the guide.
3. Reduce the chat share to one or two volunteers.

Do not cut the activity, knowledge graph, or practice loop. They are the session's usable value.

## If you are two minutes ahead

Walk once through the React example from target → prerequisite map → frontier → proof task. Ask which part supplies evidence of learning. Do not introduce another concept.

## If the audience challenges the foundations-first idea

Say:

> A project can create motivation and reveal gaps. The point is not to postpone all projects until every foundation is complete. The point is to use the project as a destination and diagnostic, then build the missing foundations deliberately instead of repeatedly attacking an overloaded task.

This prevents a false choice between “study fundamentals forever” and “learn everything through an unguided project.”

## If someone asks whether AI can be trusted as the mapper

Say:

> Treat the AI-generated map as a first hypothesis. Compare it with trusted curricula, official documentation, experienced practitioners, and—most importantly—your performance. If the step is repeatedly too hard, the map is missing something.

## If someone says they can simply use AI to produce the work

Say:

> That may be a valid production strategy. It is not evidence that you acquired the capability. Decide which outcome you need: finished output, personal capability, or both. Do not confuse them.

# 8. Evidence and source guardrails

- Primary source: Justin Skycak, [*The Math Academy Way*](https://www.justinmath.com/books/), current 510-page working draft reviewed chapter by chapter.
- Supporting source: Justin Skycak, [*Advice on Upskilling*](https://docs.google.com/document/d/17qFY5w9uEWL4VVyJSTEmSSRyvegh_Y4N6NMSxwCmGaE/edit), working draft updated 25 August 2026, reviewed block by block. It contributes practical coaching language and examples; treat its uncited claims as the author's advice rather than settled evidence.
- Bloom's original article: [*The 2 Sigma Problem*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0013189X013006004) (1984).
- Bloom's talent-development study: [*Developing Talent in Young People*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/15009/developing-talent-in-young-people-by-benjamin-bloom/) (1985).
- A later review found smaller average effects than the familiar two-sigma figure and found designed intelligent tutoring systems nearly as effective as human tutoring in the reviewed studies: [VanLehn (2011)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00461520.2011.611369).
- A meta-analysis of 50 intelligent tutoring evaluations found a median effect of 0.66 standard deviations, with important variation by assessment and implementation: [Kulik & Fletcher (2016)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654315581420).
- Do not generalise intelligent-tutoring-system findings to ordinary AI chat without qualification.
- Present Math Academy as a detailed implementation of learning-science ideas, not as independent proof of every claim it makes about its own product.
- Translate school and mathematics research to professional learning carefully. The activity is a practical application of the principles, not a validated professional-training intervention.

# 9. One-page audience handout

## Turn a learning goal into a next capability

```text
1. TARGET
   What real result do I want to use this skill to produce?

2. EVIDENCE
   What would prove I can do it?

3. MAP
   What are the prerequisite skills? Which can I already demonstrate?

4. FRONTIER
   Which skill is challenging but has its direct prerequisites in place?

5. ATTEMPT
   What can I practise in 25 minutes after one useful example?

6. FEEDBACK
   What criteria will expose the most important gap?

7. PROOF
   What fresh task can I do without the original answer?

8. RETURN
   After which stable cue will I retrieve and use it again?
```

Remember:

> Do not count “AI explained it” as learning. Count what you can do without the explanation.
