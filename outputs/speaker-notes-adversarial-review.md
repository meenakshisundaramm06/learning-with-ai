# Speaker notes: adversarial review

## Verdict

Ready for one timed read-through. The script now supports a natural delivery, but no written script can prove that it sounds like the presenter until the presenter reads it aloud once.

## What I tried to break

### 1. Does it sound like an article being read aloud?

**Failure found:** The first version used polished summaries and terms such as “hypothesis,” “retrieve the move,” and “underlying move.” They sounded written.

**Change made:** Replaced them with plain spoken lines such as “the map is only a first guess,” “remember what to do,” and “I have to do it myself.” Most spoken blocks stay between 40 and 65 words, so there are frequent places to look up at the audience.

### 2. Does it assume the presenter already knows how to expand each point?

**Failure found:** Cue cards were too thin for a screen with little visible information. The presenter would have had to invent the explanation during the meeting.

**Change made:** Every case now has a full word-for-word script. `SAY` marks speech. `DO` marks pauses, chat questions, screen actions, and work time that should not be read aloud.

### 3. Does it feel like advice from an expert looking down on the audience?

**Failure found:** A framework-heavy version could make the presenter's own procrastination feel like an artificial opening.

**Change made:** The talk begins with the presenter's real React course pile and stays in first person. The audience meets the presenter as another learner. The accessible filter panel remains the main example from overload, through the skill map, to practice and proof.

### 4. Does the talk overclaim Bloom's study or AI?

**Failure found:** “AI solves the two-sigma problem” would be an unsupported promise.

**Change made:** The script states what Bloom reported, adds a short limit, and treats tutoring as a design question. It says that AI can support a responsive loop, not that AI automatically recreates tutoring or guarantees the same result.

### 5. Could the audience use AI and still avoid learning?

**Failure found:** Asking AI for a map and examples can become another form of passive consumption.

**Change made:** Each AI use now leads back to an attempt. The script asks AI to inspect work without rewriting it, find one gap, give a smaller correction task, and test the learner on a fresh task. The closing measure is what the learner can do after the explanation leaves the screen.

### 6. Does the activity fit its twelve-minute slot?

**Failure found:** The first activity plan used the full twelve minutes for silent work and then added instructions and sharing on top.

**Change made:** Silent work now totals eight minutes: one minute for the outcome, three for the map, two to challenge it, and two for the proof task. Spoken instructions take about three minutes. A short chat share uses the final minute. A six-box fallback protects the activity if AI is slow.

### 7. Will the full session reach 45 minutes without rushing?

The verbatim speech is about 2,870 words. At 105–115 words per minute, that is about 25–27 minutes of speech. The activity supplies eight minutes of work. Chat prompts, replies, pauses, transitions, and the final calendar action supply the remaining time. The chapter clock still totals 45 minutes.

**Risk that remains:** Fast speech or short chat replies could finish the session early. Long replies could push it over. Keep the twelve-minute activity fixed. If time runs short, read one reply instead of three. If time runs long, ask one person how their proof task differs from the course or resource they first planned to use.

## Final read-aloud test

Do one timed run before the meeting. Do not rehearse for polish. Mark only:

1. A sentence you naturally say in a different way.
2. A place where you run out of breath.
3. A term you would need to explain.
4. The clock time when Case 07 begins.

If Case 07 begins between minute 30 and minute 32, keep the script as it is. If it begins later, shorten audience reply-reading before you cut the core reasoning.
